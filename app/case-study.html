01

The problem

Americans spent about $109 billion on lottery tickets in 2025, and about half of adults play in a given year. A $2 Powerball ticket has a 1 in 292.2 million chance at the jackpot, and the money is gone the moment it loses. Meanwhile $500 in a savings account at 4% earns $1.67 a month, which is too small to feel.

The UK solved this in 1956 with Premium Bonds: savers keep their money, and the interest it would have earned is paid out as prizes. More than 22 million people hold them. In the U.S., credit unions ran a version called Save to Win, but it stayed local and branch-based. Nobody has built the national, mobile version.

02

The product

  1. Add money.From $1. Sign in with email; there's no crypto wallet or seed phrase to manage.
  2. It's held in U.S. Treasury bills.A cash drawer pays most withdrawals instantly; the rest earns interest.
  3. Interest becomes prizes.85% of it is paid out in a monthly draw, 15% runs the service. Every dollar saved is an entry.
  4. Your deposit never moves.Win or not, it stays yours, and you can take it out anytime.

  • The app is a static site with a strict security policy: pages only run their own code and only talk to the services above. Savers sign in with email through Coinbase, which keeps the account's keys and pays the network fees.
  • Payments go through Coinbase's Apple Pay checkout, started by a small server that only creates a payment for a signed-in saver, into their own account.
  • One transaction sets everything up: the test dollars, the T-bill fund, the vault, and a funded Chainlink subscription.

04

The hard part: making the draw scale

Entries are fair only if they count time: $100 saved for the whole month should count the same as $200 saved for half of it, so a big deposit the night before a draw doesn't win an unfair edge. Each saver's entries are their balance multiplied by the seconds it was held.

The first version worked like a spreadsheet. When a round closed, it went down the list of every saver, worked out their entries, and wrote down a running total, so a random number could find its spot on the line. That costs a little for every saver, every draw. At 1,000 savers, closing a draw took 47 million gas; at 2,000 it took 94 million, more than fits in a single transaction. The draw would simply stop working at a few hundred savers.

The fix: a sum tree

Every saver gets one leaf of a binary tree, and every branch stores the totals of the leaves below it. Entries can't be stored directly (they grow every second), but they can be written as two numbers that only change when someone deposits or withdraws:

entries = L × balance  −  Σ ( change × seconds since the round began )
          (L = the round's length)

So each branch keeps a total of balances and a total of that second term. The root holds everyone's entries; a deposit updates one path from a leaf to the root; and picking a winner is one walk from the root down, choosing left or right at each branch. All of it costs O(log n): one more step each time the number of savers doubles.

One more problem: after a round closes, the random number takes a minute or so to arrive, and people keep saving in the meantime. Those changes must not alter the closed round's entries. The vault keeps two copies of the second term, one for each round parity. While the new round runs in one, changes are also recorded in the closed round's copy as if they happened at its close, which cancels out exactly, so its entries stay frozen until the winners are paid. Old rounds are cleared lazily: a branch stamped with an older round simply reads as zero, so starting a round costs nothing.

Adding the loyalty boost

Dollars that stay in earn more entries: 1.5x after 3 rounds, 2x after 6, 3x after 12. A withdrawal takes the newest dollars first, which makes this the same as counting the lowest balance over the last 3, 6 and 12 rounds. The catch is that boosts step up on their own as rounds pass, with no transaction to update the tree. So each branch also keeps a small ring of the dollars below it by the round they arrived, for the last 13 rounds. A dollar's age is read from the ring, anything older is simply "old" (3x), and the root still holds everyone's boosted entries, so closing a draw stays one read.

05

Fair, and anyone can check it

Each draw's random number comes from Chainlink VRF with a cryptographic proof that the blockchain checks before accepting it, so nobody, including Plutos, can choose or predict it. The number is mixed with each prize's position to pick one spot on the line per prize.

The Draws page shows it happening: everyone's stretch of the line, the random number arriving, and each prize landing. Your browser reads the line from the blockchain, re-does the math itself, and checks that it gets the same winners the blockchain recorded.

06

Safe

The promises (your deposit is always yours, prizes only come from interest, the vault always holds what it owes) are written as tests that random programs try to break: 256 sequences of 150 random actions, checked after every action, ending with every saver withdrawing everything and getting every cent back. Those tests found one real issue, a rounding gap of a millionth of a dollar, which is now fixed. An independent scanner reports no high-severity findings. Read the details.

07

Runs by itself

A keeper checks every 10 minutes. When a round is over it closes it, which asks Chainlink for a random number. Chainlink answers by itself, and in most draws the winners are paid in the same step. When a draw is too big for that, the keeper pays them on its next check. It also keeps the cash drawer topped up. Everything the keeper does is something anyone is allowed to do, so if it ever stops, anyone can finish a draw.

08

What's next

  • The legal structure first, likely with a bank or credit union partner, since prize-linked savings is clearly allowed for them in about 34 states.
  • A real Treasury bill fund or bank partner in place of the test fund, plus identity checks.
  • Two independent security audits, a multisig owner with a time delay, and monitoring.
  • A capped pilot with real deposits in select states.
